    How to rent a flat in Spain: a complete guide for Ukrainians and Russian speakers

    Krepko · updated 13 November 2026 · ~7 min read
    Renting a flat in Spain is harder than it seems: the owner asks for an NIE, proof of income and a guarantor, and the terms of the contract and the deposit are governed by the LAU law. Below we go through which documents you will be asked for, what to check in the contrato de arrendamiento before signing and which landlord demands you are entitled not to meet.     Why renting in Spain works differently

    Renting in Spain is governed by the Ley de Arrendamientos Urbanos (LAU) (Urban Leases Act) — it protects both the owner and the tenant. The difference from Ukraine and Russia is that verbal agreements are not relied on here: the rights and obligations of the parties are set out in the contract, and in a dispute the court decides first and foremost on the basis of the text you signed.

    A Spanish landlord is extremely cautious: they need every guarantee that you will pay. If you are not an EU citizen, this can become a problem — many owners will simply turn down foreigners without a Spanish contact (a guarantor). If you are planning to buy property in Spain in the future, renting gives you time to get to know the area and work out your budget without risk.

    Types of rental contract

    Contrato de arrendamiento de vivienda (long-term rental)

    The main type of contract — for a permanent home, with full protection under the LAU: the term in the contract can be anything, but if it is shorter than 5 years (7 if the owner is a company), the contract is extended every year up to that term unless you yourself decide to end it. The rent is fixed for the first year; after that it can be raised by no more than the IRAV index published by the INE (National Statistics Institute). The owner can terminate the contract early only for reasons expressly named in the law.

    Contrato de arrendamiento temporal (temporary rental)

    This is signed when the home is needed for a period of time rather than as a permanent residence (studies, seasonal work), and the LAU protection here is weaker. It suits you if you have not yet decided whether you are staying for long, but you will usually have to pay more than for a long-term rental, and when the term ends the owner can ask you to vacate the flat without much formality.

    Documents the landlord will ask for

    Before the contract is signed, you will be asked for documents that confirm your identity and ability to pay:

    • NIE or TIE — the foreigner identity number (compulsory for any legal transaction in Spain)
    • Passport — a copy of the main page and the page with the visa
    • Proof of income — a tax return (Declaración de la Renta), a certificate from your employer or bank statements showing that your income is 3 times the rent
    • Certificate of no outstanding debts — a credit history check (if you have only recently arrived in Spain, you may not have one)
    • Guarantor (aval) — a person with income in Spain (not necessarily a Spaniard) who will stand surety for you, or a bank guarantee

    Deposit (fianza) and guarantees

    Fianza (deposit) — the security you pay when you move in. By law, in a long-term contract it is 1 month's rent; on top of that the owner may ask for an additional guarantee — in a contract of up to 5 years, no more than 2 months' rent. The money is returned after you move out if the flat is undamaged and the payments arrived on time. The owner is entitled to keep part of the deposit only for real damage — not for faded wallpaper and not for ordinary wear and tear.

    Check that the deposit is registered: in many regions, including Catalonia, Valencia, Madrid and Andalusia, the owner is required to lodge the fianza with a public register (registro de depósitos). It is lodged after signing — ask for a copy of the receipt: if they later refuse to return the money, this is your main evidence.

    Aval bancario (bank guarantee) — a substitute for a guarantor: the bank guarantees your payments in writing. The bank charges €200–400 for this service, but owners are noticeably more willing to accept you with one. It is arranged through an account with a Spanish bank — if you do not have an account yet, we handle opening the account and accompany you to the branch.

    What you must check in the contract

    • Term — in a long-term contract: if it is shorter than 5 years (7 if the owner is a company), under the LAU you are entitled to extend the contract every year up to that term
    • Rent and payment terms — when to pay, how to pay, where to transfer the money
    • IPC indexation — whether the landlord can raise the rent, and by what percentage
    • IBI (property tax) — check who pays it. It should be paid by the owner, but sometimes they try to shift it onto the tenant. This is unlawful
    • Utility bills — the contract should state who pays for electricity, water and gas, and who pays for the building's upkeep (cuota de comunidad)
    • Notice periods — how much notice must be given to terminate the contract (in a long-term contract: you must give 30 days' notice; the owner cannot terminate the contract at will during the first 5 years)
    • What comes with the flat — a list of furniture and appliances and their condition (an inventory on moving in and moving out is very important)
    Tip: do not rely on verbal agreements — they are hard to prove in a dispute. Everything you have agreed with the owner (repairs, furniture, permission for pets, move-in date) should be written into the contract and signed by both parties.

    Utility bills: what, who and how much

    Cuota de comunidad (building maintenance fee) — a monthly payment for cleaning the entrance and common areas, the lift and security. The tenant pays it only if the contract says so and states the annual amount; if there is no such clause, the owner pays it — clarify this before signing. The amount depends on the building and the region (from €50 to €300 a month).

    Agua (water), electricidad (electricity), gas — paid by the tenant. Each service has its own contract with a supplier; bills come monthly or quarterly. When you move in, check whose name they are in: if they are in the previous tenant's name, you will have to transfer them into your name.

    IBI (property tax) — paid by the owner. A clause stating that IBI falls on the tenant appears in contracts regularly and is contrary to the law. If you see one, demand that it be removed or look for another flat.

    It is also worth thinking about home insurance (seguro de hogar): basic tenant liability insurance is inexpensive and often comes to the rescue if you flood the neighbours or an appliance breaks down.

    Tenant rights: what a landlord cannot do

    • Cannot demand cash under the table — all payments must be official
    • Cannot enter the flat without your consent — advance notice is not a substitute for consent; without consent, entry is possible only by court order, except in emergencies
    • Cannot keep the deposit for ordinary wear and tear — only for real damage
    • Cannot cut off utilities — this is a criminal offence
    • Cannot demand a credit history report from Russia or Ukraine — Spain has a different system, and such reports carry no legal weight

    Where to look for a home

    Idealista.com — the country's largest portal: listings from agencies and owners. Filter by price, area and type of contract (larga duración — long-term), and skip listings without photos or with a price noticeably below the market.

    Fotocasa.es — a competitor of Idealista, with a similar interface and often the same listings.

    Habitaclia.com — another large portal, especially good for finding temporary rentals.

    Facebook groups — “Russians in Barcelona”, “Ukrainians in Madrid” and similar. Prices there are often lower, but there are also more scammers: the flat is shown only on video and you are asked to transfer the deposit in advance.

    Typical problems for Ukrainians and Russian speakers

    No Spanish guarantor (aval)

    The way out is a bank guarantee (aval bancario) or an agency that acts as an intermediary and itself stands surety for the tenant. If the bank asks for documents you do not have, message us — we will look at the list and tell you which of them we can take on.

    No credit history in Spain

    This is normal for foreigners. Instead, present bank statements, proof of income and your passport. It helps if you have a Spanish bank account open — it adds trust. As soon as you move in, get your municipal registration (padrón) at the rental address: without it you cannot get a tarjeta sanitaria (health card) or access most social services.

    The owner asks you to pay several months in advance

    A deposit and the first month in advance are normal practice. But being asked to pay more — and before you have even viewed the flat and signed the contract — almost always means fraud. Insist on viewing the flat and signing the contract, and transfer money only after that.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which documents will a landlord in Spain ask for?
    A landlord usually asks for an NIE or TIE, a passport, proof of income (your income should be three times the rent), a certificate of no outstanding debts and often a guarantor (aval) or a bank guarantee.
    What is a fianza and how much is it?
    A fianza is a deposit: in a long-term contract, by law it equals 1 month's rent, and an additional guarantee on top, in a contract of up to 5 years, is no more than 2 months' rent. The money is returned after you move out if the flat is undamaged and you paid on time; in many regions, including Catalonia, Valencia, Madrid and Andalusia, the deposit is registered with a public register.
    How does a long-term contract differ from a temporary one?
    A contrato de arrendamiento de vivienda is signed for a permanent home and is protected by the LAU: if the term is shorter than 5 years (7 if the owner is a company), you are entitled to extend the contract every year up to that term, and the rent can be raised by no more than the IRAV index. A contrato temporal is for when the home is needed for a period of time rather than as a permanent residence; the protection is weaker, the rent is usually higher, and eviction when the term ends is simpler.
    Who pays the utility bills when renting in Spain?
    Usually the tenant pays for electricity, water, gas and internet, and the landlord pays the IBI (property tax) and the building's shared costs (cuota de comunidad). The exact split is set out in the contract.
