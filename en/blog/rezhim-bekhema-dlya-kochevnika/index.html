    Beckham Law regime for digital nomads: 24% and 6 months to file Modelo 149

    Krepko · 24 November 2026 · ~9 min read
    In short. A nomad who works remotely under an employment contract can opt for the Beckham Law regime: an employee with a visa for remote work is expressly named in art. 93.1 of the IRPF Law. The requirements are not to have been a tax resident of Spain in the 5 years before the year of the move and not to receive income through a permanent establishment in Spain. The Modelo 149 application is filed no later than 6 months from the start date of activity shown in your registration with the Spanish social security system, the Seguridad Social (art. 116 of the IRPF Regulation); after this deadline, ordinary IRPF applies. Under the regime, salary up to €600,000 a year is taxed at 24% and anything above that amount at 47%, the return is filed on Modelo 151, and the regime applies in the year you become resident and for 5 more years.

    The Beckham Law regime is a special tax regime for people who have become tax residents of Spain because they moved here to work: salary is taxed at 24% rather than on the progressive IRPF scale. Since 2023 the law has expressly named the case of a holder of a visa for remote work, that is, the digital nomad visa.

    Below we look at which nomads the regime is open to, from what date the 6 months for the Modelo 149 application are counted, what exactly is taxed at 24% and what happens if the deadline is missed. How to get the visa itself is explained in our article about the digital nomad visa. We checked the rules on 24 November 2026 against the BOE and the website of the tax office (AEAT).

    Which nomads can use the regime?

    The regime is set out in art. 93 of the IRPF Law (Ley 35/2006). From 1 January 2023 the article was rewritten by the Startups Law (Ley 28/2022): the period during which you must not have been resident in Spain was shortened from 10 to 5 years, and remote workers were added. The requirements are:

    • 5 years outside Spanish tax residence. You were not a tax resident of Spain in any of the five tax periods (calendar years) before the year of the move. As a general rule, a person becomes resident if they spend more than 183 days in Spain in a calendar year (art. 9.1.a LIRPF).
    • A move for a reason named in the law. For a nomad, this is an employment contract: the requirement is met if the work is done remotely and exclusively by computer and telecommunications means, and employees with a visa for remote work under Ley 14/2013 are named expressly. The move must take place in the first year of the regime or in the year before.
    • No income through a permanent establishment in Spain (establecimiento permanente), except for an entrepreneur with a favourable ENISA report and a highly qualified professional who works with startups or in training and R&D.

    The main limitation concerns freelance nomads. The law speaks of employees (por cuenta ajena), and the IRPF Regulation (art. 113.2) specifies that, while the regime applies, only three types of business activity are allowed: activity recognised as entrepreneurial by an ENISA report, services to startups by a highly qualified professional, and training or R&D. Ordinary freelancing as an autónomo (self-employed) is not on this list. If you have both an employment contract and freelance clients, the requirements need to be checked before you file, not after.

    A separate situation is that of Ukrainians who already live in Spain under temporary protection and are switching to the nomad visa. If you were a tax resident here in any of the five years before the year of the move, the first requirement is not met. More details are in our article on switching from temporary protection to the nomad visa.

    Family members can also opt for the regime: a spouse (or the other parent of the children if there is no marriage) and children under 25, or of any age if they have a disability. The condition is that the sum of their tax bases in each year of the regime is lower than the main taxpayer's tax base (art. 93.3 LIRPF). Each person files their own Modelo 149, and the main taxpayer files first.

    From what date are the 6 months for Modelo 149 counted?

    Under art. 116.1.a of the IRPF Regulation (RD 439/2007), the application is filed within six months at most from the start date of activity shown:

    • in the registration (alta) with the Spanish Seguridad Social;
    • or in the document under which you remain in the social security system of your own country, if this option applies to you;
    • or, if registration with the Seguridad Social is not compulsory, in a document confirming the date you started work.

    The count starts not from your entry into Spain, not from the date of the visa and not from receiving your TIE card (foreigner identity card), but from the start date of activity in these documents. If the registration shows March, the application must be filed by the corresponding day in September. Family members have their own deadline: 6 months from their entry or the main taxpayer's deadline, whichever is later.

    A missed deadline. The Regulation calls the 6 months a maximum period (plazo máximo); art. 116 provides for no extension or reinstatement. If the application is not filed on time, once you become a tax resident you pay IRPF under the general rules: your income from all over the world is taxed, wherever it was received (art. 2 LIRPF), on the progressive scale, and you file the Renta return and the foreign assets return Modelo 720 if these obligations apply to you.

    Another trap is Modelo 147. This is a notification to the tax office about your move, so that your employer starts withholding tax as for a resident (art. 89.B of the Regulation). Anyone who has used this procedure can no longer opt for the Beckham Law regime (art. 116.3).

    How is Modelo 149 filed and which documents are needed?

    1. NIF and tax registration
    You must have a NIF (for a foreign national, the NIE) and be on the tax register (Censo de Obligados Tributarios). If you are not, you first file a registration return, for example Modelo 030.
    2. Documents sent separately
    First, the documents are uploaded through the service “Aportar documentación necesaria para optar por el régimen especial” (submitting the documents needed to opt for the special regime) and you receive a registration number for the submission. Without this number, Modelo 149 cannot be completed.
    3. Filing Modelo 149
    Online only, with an electronic certificate or Cl@ve PIN. For a remote worker who did not move at the employer's instruction, the corresponding tick goes in box 43.
    4. Confirmation from the tax office
    If everything is in order, within 10 working days the AEAT issues a document confirming that you have opted for the regime. A copy of it is given to your employer or another payer who withholds the tax.

    For a nomad working remotely as an employee, art. 119.2 of the Regulation requires:

    • a document of registration with the Spanish Seguridad Social or a document under which you remain in the social security system of your own country (if registration is not compulsory, a document showing the date you started work);
    • a residence authorisation, if you have one; for a nomad this is the visa or residence permit for remote work;
    • a certificate from your employer: that you work for them, the start date of activity according to the social security registration and the expected duration of work from Spain.

    The application can only be filed with electronic access, so it is better to get a certificate in advance. How it differs from Cl@ve is covered in our article about the certificado digital.

    What is taxed at 24% and what is not?

    Under the regime you remain an IRPF taxpayer, but the tax is calculated under the rules of the non-resident income tax (IRNR), with the specific features set out in art. 93.2 LIRPF:

    Income Rate
    Salary and other income except dividends, interest and capital gains, up to €600,000 a year 24%
    The same part of income above €600,000 47%
    Dividends, interest and capital gains from Spanish sources 19% up to €6,000, 21% up to €50,000, 23% up to €200,000, 27% up to €300,000, 30% above that

    An important detail for nomads: all salary earned during the regime is treated as received in Spain (art. 93.2.b), even if the employer is abroad and the money arrives in a foreign account. Other foreign income, for example renting out a flat back home or interest on a foreign deposit, is not taxed under the regime: according to AEAT guidance, tax under the regime is paid only on income from Spanish sources. If you also pay tax on your salary in another country, the deduction for double taxation is limited (art. 114.2 of the Regulation).

    The withholding rate on salary under the regime is 24%, and on the amount above €600,000 from a single payer in a year it is 47%. The annual return is filed not on the usual Renta form but on Modelo 151, within the same deadlines that are set each year for the ordinary IRPF return.

    There are also consequences beyond IRPF. Someone who has opted for the regime does not file the Modelo 720 foreign assets return (AEAT guidance on Modelo 720); family members who have not opted for the regime and are tax residents may be required to file it. Wealth tax (Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio) under the regime is paid only on assets in Spain (art. 93.1 LIRPF).

    Who may find the regime unfavourable?

    Whether the regime pays off has to be calculated on each person's own figures, but the law has rules that can make the result worse than ordinary IRPF:

    • No reductions to the tax base. Under IRNR rules, the full amount of income is taxed, without the reductions available in ordinary IRPF (art. 24.1 of the IRNR Law). On a modest salary, a 24% rate “from the first euro” can produce a higher tax than the progressive scale.
    • Income items are not offset against each other. Income for the year is added together, but no offsetting between items is allowed: a loss on one item of income does not reduce the tax on another (art. 93.2.c LIRPF).
    • No resident status under agreements for the avoidance of double taxation. According to AEAT guidance, people under the regime are not considered residents of Spain for the purposes of these agreements.
    • You cannot leave and come back. You can give up the regime only in November–December, with effect from the following year, and if you breach the requirements you are excluded from the year in which this happened and must report it within a month. In both cases you can no longer return to the regime (arts. 117 and 118 of the Regulation).

    That is why we compare both options before Modelo 149 is filed: after that, you can change your mind only by renouncing the regime, with no right to return. How ordinary income tax works is explained in our article about IRPF in Spain.

    How many years does the regime last?

    The regime applies in the year you become a tax resident and in the 5 following years, up to 6 calendar years in total (art. 115 of the Regulation). The year in which residence is acquired is the first calendar year in which, after the move, you spent more than 183 days in Spain. If you moved in the autumn and spent fewer than 183 days here that year, the first year of the regime will be the following one. If your work from Spain ends but you remain resident in the same year, the end is reported within a month on the same Modelo 149.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I get the Beckham Law regime with a digital nomad visa?
    Yes, if you work remotely under an employment contract: an employee with a visa for remote work is expressly named in art. 93.1.b LIRPF. You must also not have been a tax resident of Spain in the 5 years before the year of the move, and you must file Modelo 149 no later than 6 months from the start date of activity in your Seguridad Social registration.
    I am a freelance nomad (autónomo). Can I use the Beckham Law regime?
    As a rule, no. The law speaks of employees, and the IRPF Regulation (art. 113.2) allows only three types of activity under the regime: activity recognised as entrepreneurial by an ENISA report, services to startups by a highly qualified professional, and training or R&D. Ordinary freelancing is not on this list.
    From what date are the 6 months for Modelo 149 counted?
    From the start date of activity shown in your registration with the Spanish Seguridad Social, or in the document under which you remain in the social security system of your own country (art. 116.1.a of the IRPF Regulation). The date of entry, of the visa or of receiving your TIE does not affect the deadline. For family members, the deadline is 6 months from their entry or the main taxpayer's deadline, whichever is later.
    What happens if I have missed the 6 months for the application?
    The Regulation calls the 6 months a maximum period, and art. 116 provides no way to reinstate it. Once you become a tax resident, you pay IRPF under the general rules: your worldwide income is taxed on the progressive scale, and you file the Renta return and the Modelo 720 foreign assets return if these obligations apply to you.
    Is only income in Spain taxed under the Beckham Law regime?
    Not quite. All salary earned during the regime is treated as received in Spain, even if the employer is abroad (art. 93.2.b LIRPF), and is taxed at 24% up to €600,000 and at 47% above that amount. Other foreign income, for example renting out a flat back home, is not taxed under the regime: tax is paid on income from Spanish sources.
    Which tax return is filed under the Beckham Law regime?
    Not the usual Renta but Modelo 151, within the same deadlines that are set each year for the IRPF return. According to AEAT guidance, someone who has opted for the regime does not file the Modelo 720 foreign assets return.
